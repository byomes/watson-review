CHAPTER 1 — "The Thing Nobody Worries About"

Role in the book: pre-guardrail setup chapter. It runs BEFORE the four guardrails are introduced, so it must NOT anchor to Scripture and must NOT include field-engagement quotes or outside citations — that's deliberate, by design. Keep it personal and observational. The whole chapter's job is to get the reader nodding along about a tool they already trust completely (spell-check), then pull the rug out at the very end by pointing at the tool they don't yet realize deserves more scrutiny (AI).

Required word count: 3,000+ words minimum. Do not stop short of 3,000 — if the material runs thin, develop the reflective/transitional passages between stories rather than padding with repetition.

REQUIRED CONTENT, IN THIS ORDER:

1. Open on spell-check as the hook. Establish its pedigree: it originated at Stanford's AI Lab in 1971, went mainstream to ordinary people through Microsoft Word in the 1990s, and has now been in constant daily use for 50+ years — 30+ years in ordinary hands — with zero moral panic, zero sermons warning about it, zero parent ever worried their kid was "becoming dependent" on it. Set up the question the chapter will answer: why did this tool earn total, unexamined trust, and what does that tell us about the tool that hasn't?

2. The "receive" story. I mistype "receive" almost every week (i before e, e before i — I never remember the rule). Spell-check fixes it instantly, and I feel zero guilt about it, "because fixing my spelling isn't altering or changing the meaning of what I'm trying to communicate at all."

3. The Dreamstone Chronicles story. Writing fiction with invented words, made-up slang, and constructed spells for a fantasy world. Grammar-check kept flagging all of it as wrong. I overruled it every time, because "I know the difference between what I'm trying to express and the cold calculated precision of the only right way to say something." Use this to introduce the idea that the human stays the final authority — the tool checks, it doesn't decide.

4. The polish-vs-generate argument. This is close to publishable as-is, keep it near-verbatim: "Spell check and grammar check do not generate ideas, they only polish or double-check the human idea, which is why we don't hold any moral ambiguity over them." Extend this into a short passage connecting it to academic integrity and authorship — a paper checked by spell-check is still entirely the student's paper; a paper written by AI raises a different question entirely, because now the ideas themselves, not just the punctuation, may not be the human's own.

5. The "box" argument — this is the sharpest line in the chapter, build the chapter's turn around it. Core idea, keep near-verbatim: "the danger of AI is always the tool doing more than it's asked to do... the original spell check never did more than it was asked. So it remained in its box, and inside of its box is what kept it safe." Unpack this: spell-check could never have talked you into an idea, never suggested you write something you didn't intend, never had an opinion about your topic. It stayed exactly inside its lane. That containment — not the tool's simplicity, but its boundedness — is what made 50 years of total trust possible. Modern AI is the first tool in this category that routinely steps outside the box it was handed.

6. The email auto-suggest story. My email client started pre-suggesting my own habitual sign-off phrase as a predictive-text suggestion — regardless of whether the email was even about answering a question. It was pattern-matching my verbal tic, not my actual logic, and it was "completely unrelated to the actual content" of what I was writing in that moment. Use this to introduce a second, quieter danger beyond generating ideas: predictive text pre-empting thought itself, nudging you toward a phrase before you've finished deciding what you actually want to say. This is a small, almost comic example, but it's the first crack in the box — a tool anticipating you instead of merely checking you.

7. Closing guardrails-frame beat. Land the chapter on this line, near-verbatim: "We didn't need guardrails when we had spell check because spell check wasn't capable of crossing into danger territory." This is the thesis of the whole book compressed into one sentence — name it as such.

8. Final transition line into Chapter 2/3, verbatim, as the very last line of the chapter: "So if it's not the tool we should fear, what is it we really fear?"

DO NOT INCLUDE (explicitly cut by me from earlier drafts, keep them out): the "Yomes" vs. "Homes" surname-autocorrect story, and the general story about autocorrect flagging Greek/theological terms. Both were cut to keep the chapter tight — don't reintroduce them.
